\section{Scientific context and motivation}

Mean-field control (MFC) provides a framework for controlling large populations of interacting agents under a common objective, in problems where the evolution or the cost of each agent depends not only on its own state and action, but also on the distribution of the population. In a computer network, for instance, the probability that a machine becomes compromised depends on the proportion of compromised machines, while a central controller allocates protection resources. Modelling every agent individually leads to a state space whose dimension grows with the population size. In the mean-field limit, the empirical distribution is replaced by the law of a representative agent, yielding a control problem whose formulation no longer depends explicitly on the number of agents \cite{motte2022mean}. This reduction makes large systems tractable, at the price of a measure-valued state variable.

Most numerical methods for MFC assume that the transition dynamics and the cost functions are known. They rely on dynamic programming, on forward--backward systems of Hamilton--Jacobi--Bellman and Fokker--Planck equations, or on direct differentiation through the population dynamics \cite{pham2018bellman, carmona2018probabilistic, lauriere2021numerical}. In each case the value function and the population distribution evolve jointly, so knowledge of the model is central to both the analysis and the numerical solution.

Policy-gradient methods are attractive here because they optimize a parametrized policy directly and accommodate randomized policies, continuous actions, and neural-network parametrizations. In a classical Markov decision process, REINFORCE expresses the gradient of the expected cost using only sampled returns and the score of the policy \cite{williams1992reinforce}; the representation is model-free because, conditionally on the current state and action, the transition kernel does not depend on the policy parameters. This property no longer holds in mean-field control. The population law is generated by the policy itself, so changing the parameters changes the population flow, which then changes the policies, costs, and transition kernels evaluated at subsequent times. The classical policy score accounts for the direct effect of the parameters on the sampled actions, but not for their indirect effect through the population law, and REINFORCE applied without modification is therefore biased.

A recent method addresses this difficulty in discrete-time, finite-state MFC by perturbing the logits of the population vector and deriving an additional likelihood-ratio correction \cite{meunier2026model}, establishing that fully model-free policy gradients are possible in this setting. The construction relies on one logit coordinate per state, however, and is therefore tied to finite state spaces: it does not extend to a continuous state space, where the population state is a probability measure with no canonical finite-dimensional parametrization.

The motivation of this work is to develop a perturbation framework that separates the policy-gradient construction from the representation of the population law. In finite state spaces, probability-preserving perturbations can be defined directly on the simplex; in continuous state spaces, they can instead be constructed through transport maps. The remaining question is then one of representation: a probability measure must be described through a finite collection of relevant functionals, such as moments, interaction fields, or learned features, and the score entering the gradient estimator is associated with the joint law of these functionals rather than with the population measure directly. This connects model-free MFC with representation learning on spaces of probability measures, and provides a common framework for discrete- and continuous-state problems.

\section{Objectives of the internship}

The central objective of the internship was to investigate whether policy-gradient methods for discrete-time mean-field control could be made fully model-free without relying on the finite-state logit representation of \cite{meunier2026model}, that is, using only simulated trajectories, policy evaluations and observed costs, without differentiating or explicitly knowing the transition dynamics.

The first objective was theoretical: to identify the additional dependence created by the population flow, to construct a perturbation of this flow for which a likelihood-ratio representation can be derived, to show that the perturbed gradient approximates the gradient of the original MFC objective as the perturbation vanishes, and to study the bias--variance trade-off it introduces. The second was to develop constructions adapted to both discrete and continuous state spaces: on the simplex, a probability-preserving perturbation replacing the logit one; in continuous state spaces, a perturbation through transport maps whose score is expressed through a finite-dimensional representation of the perturbed law. This second case raises a related representation-learning question, namely which functionals of the population distribution carry the information required by the dynamics, the costs and the policy. The third objective was algorithmic: converting these representations into practical Monte Carlo estimators, including an estimator of the sensitivity of the population flow, while reusing simulated trajectories across the horizon, controlling computational and memory costs, and selecting the perturbation scales, possibly adaptively.

Finally, the numerical objective was to evaluate the resulting methods against standard REINFORCE and the finite-state MF-REINFORCE method under matched simulation budgets, on four finite-state and two continuous-state benchmarks. Beyond final performance, the study examines gradient bias and variance, sensitivity to the perturbation scale, computational cost, and robustness to particle approximations of the population flow.


\section{Contributions}

The main contribution of this work is a perturbation-based framework for model-free policy gradients in discrete-time mean-field control. The central idea is to randomize the population argument so that its dependence on the policy parameters is transferred to the distribution of the perturbation. The gradient of the perturbed objective then decomposes into the usual policy-score term and an additional population-score term, both of which admit likelihood-ratio representations and are therefore estimable from simulated trajectories without differentiating the transition kernel. The accompanying theory provides the assurances needed for the method to be meaningful: the perturbed population laws remain controlled perturbations of the original flow, the perturbed objectives and gradients converge back to their unperturbed counterparts as the perturbation scale vanishes, the Monte Carlo estimators are unbiased or consistent for the corresponding perturbed gradients, and bias--variance results quantify the finite-sample trade-off that the scale creates.

\paragraph{A transport view of perturbed population flows.}
We formulate perturbations directly at the level of probability measures. Given the population law $\mu_t^\theta=\P_{X_t^\theta}$, we introduce a random transport map $T_t^\lambda$ and replace the population argument by $M_t^{\lambda,\theta} = T_t^\lambda\sharp \mu_t^\theta$, where the perturbation scale $\lambda$ controls the distance between the original and perturbed population laws. In continuous state spaces this includes random maps of the form $T_t^\lambda(\omega,x)=x+\lambda f_t(\omega,x)$, whereas in finite state spaces the same idea becomes a perturbation on the simplex. For both discrete and continuous MFC models, the estimator is driven by a random, probability-preserving deformation of the population law rather than by a model-specific coordinate system.

\paragraph{A likelihood-ratio correction for the mean-field dependence.}
The perturbed population argument is random and its distribution depends on $\theta$ through $\mu_t^\theta$, so the gradient of the perturbed objective contains, besides the usual REINFORCE policy-score term, a score term associated with the generated population argument. This is the correction missing from the classical REINFORCE formula in MFC. The report derives the decomposition and identifies the conditions under which it can be estimated without differentiating the transition kernel, the reward, or the population recursion.

\paragraph{A finite-dimensional representation for continuous laws.}
In a general continuous state space, there is no canonical dominating measure on $\Pc(\Xc)$, so the law of $M_t^{\lambda,\theta}$ need not have a usable density. We therefore introduce a representation map $\Psi(m)=\big(F_1(m),\ldots,F_d(m)\big)$ and work with the random vector $Z_t^{\lambda,\theta}=\Psi(M_t^{\lambda,\theta})$, writing the population-score correction through the density of $Z_t^{\lambda,\theta}$ in $\R^d$. This separates the policy-gradient construction from the choice of representation: $\Psi$ may be given by exact sufficient statistics such as moments, or by interaction fields, Fourier or kernel features, or learned summaries of the population law.

\paragraph{A simplex perturbation for finite-state MFC.}
For $\mu\in\Delta_N$, we define the discrete transport perturbation $\Phi_\mu^\lambda(q)=(1-\lambda)\mu+\lambda q$ with $q\in\Delta_N$, and use $M_t^{\lambda,\theta} = (1-\lambda)\mu_t^\theta+\lambda q_t$ as the perturbed population argument, for which the simplex coordinates give an exact finite-dimensional representation. We derive the density and the score of $M_t^{\lambda,\theta}$ explicitly, obtaining a model-free gradient estimator that acts directly on probability vectors. Compared with the logit perturbation of \cite{meunier2026model}, the construction does not require strictly positive population coordinates and is expressed in the geometry of the simplex itself; under regularity assumptions the perturbed gradient converges to the unperturbed one as $\lambda\to0$, with a first-order error.

\paragraph{A recursive sensitivity estimator and practical algorithms.}
The population-score term requires the sensitivity of the population flow with respect to the policy parameters. We derive a forward recursion for this quantity, in which the sensitivity at time $t$ depends only on earlier ones, so that the whole sensitivity flow can be estimated from shared simulated trajectories. With cumulative computations this gives a simulator cost linear in the horizon, against the quadratic cost of estimating each truncated sensitivity separately. The estimators are implemented as fixed-scale and adaptive Monte Carlo algorithms, the adaptive version updating the main and auxiliary perturbation scales by comparing gradient estimates at nearby scales, so as to balance perturbation bias and estimator variance without a full environment-specific grid search.


\paragraph{Status of the contributions.}
The contributions above are at different stages, and Table~\ref{tab:contribution-status} states which. The finite-state construction is complete: every step from the perturbation estimate to the mean-square-error decomposition is proved. The continuous-state construction is complete as far as the objective, and open beyond it: the representation, the perturbation, the density and the score are established, and the perturbed objective is shown to converge, but the gradient-level counterparts are not yet proved. The continuous-state experiments are therefore run with an estimator built by analogy with the finite-state case, and the reader should treat their guarantees as an extrapolation rather than a theorem. This is the main limitation of the report and is discussed again in Section~\ref{sec:strengths-limitations}.

\section{Organization of the report}

Chapter~\ref{chapter:methodology} develops the perturbation framework and its convergence and bias--variance results, and Chapter~\ref{chapter:algorithms} turns them into fixed-scale and adaptive Monte Carlo algorithms. Chapter~\ref{chapter:experiments} presents the numerical protocol and the benchmark suite, and Chapter~\ref{chapter:discussion} discusses what the results establish, the limits of the approach and the extensions it suggests.
