\chapter{Numerical Experiments}
\label{chapter:experiments}

This chapter describes the numerical benchmark suite used to evaluate the proposed transport policy-gradient estimators. The goal is to compare REINFORCE, MF-REINFORCE, fixed-scale transport and adaptive transport under matched simulator budgets, while separating optimization performance from perturbation bias, estimator variance, and population-flow approximation error.

\section{Experimentation protocol}
\label{subsec:experimentation-protocol}

The study has three goals: to measure whether the transport estimator improves the optimization performance of model-free policy gradients in mean-field control; to separate the effect of the perturbation scale from that of the estimator geometry, by varying the transport radii and comparing exact with particle population flows; and to test whether the adaptive controller of Algorithm~\ref{alg:adaptive-transport} can select useful scales without a per-environment grid search.

A configuration is defined by the benchmark, the horizon, the algorithm, the population-flow mode, the perturbation parameters and the simulator budget, and each is repeated over five independent seeds. In a paired comparison the same seed fixes the policy initialization, the sequence of training initial laws, the Monte Carlo samples and the validation schedule, and reported curves show the mean and dispersion across seeds.

\paragraph{Algorithms compared.}
On the finite-state benchmarks we compare four methods: standard REINFORCE, which ignores the derivative of the population flow and serves as a lower-complexity baseline; the logit-perturbed MF-REINFORCE estimator of \cite{meunier2026model}; the discrete-transport estimator of Algorithm~\ref{alg:discrete-transport}; and the adaptive method of Algorithm~\ref{alg:adaptive-transport}, initialized from the same simplex estimator but allowed to update its two perturbation scales during training. On the continuous-state benchmarks, REINFORCE is compared with the continuous transport estimator and its adaptive version.

The logit perturbation level of MF-REINFORCE is not retuned here. On the two-state, cybersecurity and distribution benchmarks we use the best-performing value reported in the reference study, $\varepsilon=0.2$, $1.0$ and $2.0$ respectively; advertising is not part of that grid and we use $\varepsilon=1.0$, a value that performs reasonably on the other finite-state benchmarks. This keeps the comparison focused on the proposed estimator rather than on a new hyperparameter search for the baseline.

\paragraph{Transport grids.}
The two-state benchmark serves as the calibration experiment, over the broad grid $\lambda\in\{0.05,0.1,0.2,0.4,0.8\}$ against $\eta\in\{0.4,0.6,0.85,0.95\}$. The $\lambda$-range is deliberately wide enough to expose both small-perturbation failures, where the score correction becomes unstable, and large-perturbation failures, where the optimized objective is too biased; the $\eta$-range tests the observation that the auxiliary estimator benefits from a comparatively large radius. After this calibration the auxiliary scale is fixed at $\eta=0.85$ everywhere else and the main grid is reduced, as listed in each subsection.

Adaptive transport is initialized at $\lambda_0=0.2$, $\eta_0=0.8$, with the diagnostics of Section~\ref{sec:adaptive-transport} computed every $100$ training iterations from $4$ replications. At four replications the debiased bias estimate is also frequently non-positive, which means the bias is unresolved rather than zero; the controller therefore updates a scale only on a resolved estimate, or when the direction test detects a sign change.

\paragraph{Horizons and flow modes.}
The two-state benchmark is run at $T=2$, reproducing the reference toy problem, and at $T=5$, which tests whether the estimators degrade when sensitivity information is propagated through more steps; both MF-REINFORCE and transport are run there with exact population propagation and with a particle approximation, isolating the effect of replacing the deterministic recursion by an empirical law. The remaining benchmarks use exact propagation in the main comparison, since their finite-dimensional recursions are available exactly, and the linear--quadratic benchmark is additionally run with a particle flow.

\paragraph{Equal-budget comparison.}
All headline comparisons are made under an equal simulator-budget constraint, counted in simulated transitions per policy update and implemented by adjusting the number of main and auxiliary trajectories before training. The reference is the finite-state MF-REINFORCE configuration. Estimating the logit gradient at time $t$ requires simulating a mean-field and a perturbed trajectory over $t$ steps, for each of $n$ auxiliary samples, so one pass over the whole horizon costs $2n\sum_{t=1}^{T}t=nT(T+1)$ transitions. Algorithm~1 of \cite{meunier2026model} performs that pass inside the loop over the $B$ main trajectories, giving a per-update cost of $BT+B\,nT(T+1)$, of order $nBT^2$, which is the complexity reported there. The logit gradient is however a property of the policy and of the population flow, not of an individual trajectory, so our implementation estimates it once per update and shares it across the main batch. The reference cost is therefore
\begin{align}
    C_{\mathrm{MF}}
    =
    BT
    +
    nT(T+1),
    \label{eq:mf-reference-cost}
\end{align}
the smaller of the two, and the conservative choice here since it is the budget every other estimator must match. The transport estimator has linear horizon dependence,
\begin{align}
    C_{\mathrm{tr}}
    =
    T(B_{\mathrm{tr}}+n_{\mathrm{tr}}),
    \label{eq:transport-cost}
\end{align}
up to the common cost of particle-flow propagation when enabled. REINFORCE is given the same budget by increasing its number of trajectories, and adaptive transport the same nominal budget after its amortized checkpoint overhead. The realized budgets agree exactly across estimators on every benchmark; Appendix~\ref{appendix:budget-runtime} reports them together with the wall-clock cost, which the rule does not equalize.

\paragraph{Training and validation.}
Unless a subsection states otherwise, policies are optimized with Adam, a deterministic validation is performed every ten training iterations, and the validation policy is evaluated without exploration noise whenever the environment admits an exact population recursion. The training initial law is randomized, to test whether a policy generalizes across the relevant part of the simplex, while validation uses fixed initial laws or fixed grids; the exact objective, dynamic-programming reference or closed-form optimizer is used only after freezing the parameters. Each benchmark produces the same family of outputs: the mean validation reward against training iteration, the final objective and runtime, the policy error where a reference policy exists, the learned state or moment flow, and benchmark-specific diagnostics.

\paragraph{Estimator diagnostics and reproducibility.}
Where a benchmark provides an exact or automatic-differentiation gradient oracle, evaluation-only diagnostics are computed at saved policies: independent gradient replications at a fixed budget estimate bias, dispersion, mean-square error, cosine alignment with the oracle, and the size of the transport correction. These never enter a training update and are reported in Section~\ref{sec:theory-validation}. Every run writes its metadata, training history, final policy, runtime summary and budget estimate, and every figure and table below is regenerated from those artifacts, so the results are reproducible from the recorded seed and configuration. The suite comprises $640$ runs and roughly $94$ hours of single-core compute.

\paragraph{Hardware.}
All experiments were run on a single machine equipped with an NVIDIA RTX A4000 GPU. The policies are small, two hidden layers of width $32$ on the finite-state benchmarks and a directly parametrized affine policy on the continuous-state ones, so a training step is dominated by trajectory simulation rather than by parameter updates; the runs were accordingly executed on the CPU device of that machine, with several configurations trained concurrently. All wall-clock timings were collected under these conditions and are therefore comparable across estimators.

Table~\ref{tab:objective-summary} collects the final validation objective of every estimator on every benchmark, at the headline horizon and population-flow mode of the corresponding subsection. It is the reference point for the benchmark sections that follow, each of which discusses one problem in isolation; Section~\ref{sec:results-review} reads the table as a whole.

\section{Discrete-state space benchmarks}

\subsection{Two-State Two-Action}
\label{subsec:two-state-two-action}

We first consider the two-state two-action mean-field control problem from \cite{meunier2026model,gu2023dynamic}. Besides providing a simple sanity check, this benchmark admits an explicit optimal stationary policy and an exact recursion for the population law, enabling direct evaluation of the proposed gradient estimator against an oracle.

\paragraph{Model.}
The state and action spaces are $\Xc=\{0,1\}$ and $\Ac=\{\mathrm{ST},\mathrm{MV}\}$ respectively. $\mathrm{ST}$ leaves the current state unchanged, whereas $\mathrm{MV}$ attempts to move the agent to the other state. For $x\in\Xc$ and $a\in\Ac$, the transition kernel is given by
\begin{align}
    P(x'\mid x,a)=
    \begin{cases}
        \lambda_x\mathbf 1_{\{a=\mathrm{MV}\}},&x'\neq x, \\
        1-\lambda_x\mathbf 1_{\{a=\mathrm{MV}\}},&x'=x,
    \end{cases}
\end{align}
where $\lambda_0,\lambda_1\in(0,1)$. The transition mechanism itself does not depend on the population distribution; the mean-field interaction enters through the reward. Writing $\bar \mu=p\delta_0+(1-p)\delta_1$ the target population distribution, the running and terminal rewards are $r(x,a,\mu)=r(x,\mu)=\mathbf 1_{\{x=1\}}-\mu(1)^2-\kappa W_1(\mu,\bar\mu),\qquad g(x,\mu)=r(x,\mu)$,
where the Wasserstein term reduces to $W_1(\mu,\bar\mu)=|\mu(1)-(1-p)|$ on the binary state space endowed with the distance $d(0,1)=1$.

The optimal stationary policy sends any initial population distribution to $\bar\mu$ after one time step and preserves it thereafter. It is available in closed form:
\begin{align}
    \pi^\star(a\mid0)
    &= \frac{\lambda_0 - 1+p}{\lambda_0}\mathbf 1_{\{a=\mathrm{ST}\}} + \frac{1-p}{\lambda_0}\mathbf 1_{\{a=\mathrm{MV}\}},
    \\
    \pi^\star(a\mid 1)
    &= \frac{\lambda_1 - p}{\lambda_1}\mathbf 1_{\{a=\mathrm{ST}\}} + \frac{p}{\lambda_1}\mathbf 1_{\{a=\mathrm{MV}\}}.
\end{align}

\paragraph{Setup.}
As in the reference experiment the policy class is restricted to two Bernoulli logits $\theta=(\theta_0,\theta_1)$ and does not read the population argument, so the flow reduces to the scalar recursion \eqref{eq:twostate-population-recursion} and both the objective \eqref{eq:twostate-objective} and its maximizer $\theta^\star$ are available in closed form, with $\pi^\star(\mathrm{ST}\mid0)=0.2$ and $\pi^\star(\mathrm{ST}\mid1)=0.25$. We run $T=2$ and $T=5$, each with exact and with particle population flow, MF-REINFORCE at $\varepsilon=0.2$, the broad calibration grid $\lambda\in\{0.05,0.1,0.2,0.4,0.8\}$ against $\eta\in\{0.4,0.6,0.85,0.95\}$, and adaptive transport from $(\lambda_0,\eta_0)=(0.2,0.8)$, all at the equal budget derived from the MF-REINFORCE reference configuration $B=200$, $n=10$. Every ten iterations the policy is frozen and evaluated from the fixed law $\mu_0^{\mathrm{val}}=(0.2,0.8)$, without trajectory noise. Policy recovery is measured by the two state-wise errors
\begin{align}
    e_0(\theta) &= \left|\pi^\theta(\mathrm{ST}\mid 0)-0.2\right|, & e_1(\theta) &= \left|\pi^\theta(\mathrm{ST}\mid 1)-0.25\right|.
    \label{eq:twostate-policy-errors}
\end{align}
Appendix~\ref{appendix:spec-twostate} gives the model, the parameter values, the closed-form optimal policy and the full protocol.

\paragraph{Results.}
The exact recursion at the closed-form optimum gives $J(\theta^\star)=-3.360$ at $T=2$ and $-2.640$ at $T=5$, against $-5.000$ and $-4.532$ at the initialization $\theta=(0,0)$. Figure~\ref{fig:twostate-validation} shows that REINFORCE does not merely stall here: it moves away from the optimum from the first iterations and ends at $-14.118$ and $-31.510$, far below its own starting point. Estimators that differentiate the population flow improve instead, provided the perturbation is not too coarse: at $T=2$ every transport configuration with $\lambda\leq0.4$ ends above the initialization and only the four runs at $\lambda=0.8$ fall below it, and at $T=5$ the threshold moves to $\lambda\leq0.2$. This is the sharpest illustration in the suite of the obstruction of Chapter~\ref{chapter:methodology}: the population channel carries the sign of the useful direction, and an estimator that ignores it ascends the wrong function.

The horizon is what separates transport from MF-REINFORCE. Table~\ref{tab:twostate-horizon} reports the objective gap and the policy-recovery error at both horizons. At $T=2$ MF-REINFORCE is the more accurate estimator, recovering $\pi^\star$ to within $0.006$ against $0.014$ for the best transport configuration; at $T=5$ the ordering reverses by a wide margin, the logit policy error growing by a factor $35$ against $2.5$ for transport, with an objective gap three times smaller. This is what the per-update costs \eqref{eq:mf-reference-cost} and \eqref{eq:transport-cost} predict: at a fixed budget the quadratic horizon dependence of the logit estimator is paid for by a reduction in the number of trajectories, and the longer horizon exposes the resulting variance. Transport recovers the optimal policy to within $3.5\%$ at $T=5$.

\input{tables/twostate_horizon}


This benchmark also carries the calibration experiment announced in Section~\ref{subsec:experimentation-protocol}, reported in full in Appendix~\ref{appendix:twostate-grid}, which fixes the two design choices used on the remaining benchmarks. In the main scale the optimum is interior, at $\lambda=0.1$: below it the score of the perturbation density degrades, above it the perturbed objective is too far from the true one, and at $\lambda=0.8$ the run ends below its initialization. In the auxiliary scale the objective improves over the whole grid whenever $\lambda\leq0.1$ and peaks at $\eta=0.85$, so at this horizon the auxiliary estimator prefers a radius near the top of the range. This is the regime in which the $(n\eta^2)^{-1}$ variance term of Proposition~\ref{prop:auxiliary-sensitivity-error} dominates its $O(\eta)$ bias, which is what the small auxiliary sample sizes of the equal-budget allocation should produce. The preference is nonetheless horizon-dependent, which limits how far the calibration transfers: at $T=2$ the ordering reverses, $\eta=0.4$ being the best column at every $\lambda\geq0.1$. Since the remaining benchmarks all have $T\geq3$, $\eta=0.85$ is retained from the longer-horizon evidence.

Replacing the exact recursion by a particle approximation costs $0.32$ at $T=5$, from $-3.133$ to $-3.455$ at the best scales, about two thirds of the remaining optimality gap. The substitution is therefore not free on the simplex, in contrast with the linear--quadratic benchmark; the ordering of the estimators and the calibration in $\eta$ are nonetheless unchanged, and particle-flow transport still improves on exact-flow MF-REINFORCE.

\paragraph{Learned population flows.}
The objective and the policy error both summarize the learned policy by a single number, and Figure~\ref{fig:twostate-flows} shows what the four estimators actually do to the population. The optimal policy holds the population at the target proportions $(p,1-p)=(0.6,0.4)$ from the second step onwards, and the perturbed estimators reproduce this: MF-REINFORCE, transport and adaptive transport all drive the state-$0$ mass from its initial value $0.2$ to within a few percent of $0.6$ and hold it there. REINFORCE does the opposite. Its learned policy drives the population to the corner rather than to the target, which is what the objective value $-31.5$ and the policy error $0.469$ measure; the flow makes clear that this is not slow convergence but convergence to a qualitatively different flow.


\subsection{Cybersecurity}
\label{subsec:cybersecurity}

We next consider the cybersecurity benchmark of \cite{meunier2026model}, inspired by the mean-field game model of \cite{kolokoltsov2016mean} and its mean-field control formulation in \cite{carmona2023model}. A central planner controls the protection status of a large population of computers. Compared with the two-state toy problem, this example has a larger state space, nonlinear dependence on the population law through infection rates, and a neural-network policy depending explicitly on the population distribution.

\paragraph{Model.}
The state space is $\Xc=\{\mathrm{DI},\mathrm{DS},\mathrm{UI},\mathrm{US}\}$, where the first letter indicates whether the computer is defended or undefended and the second whether it is infected or susceptible. The action space is $\Ac=\{0,1\}$: action $0$ keeps the current protection status, while action $1$ switches between defended and undefended. To avoid confusion with the transport scale, the switching intensity is denoted by $\lambda_{\mathrm{sw}}$.

For a population law $\mu\in\Delta_4$, the infection intensities are
\begin{align}
    \iota_D(\mu)
    &=
    v_Hq_{\mathrm{inf}}^D+\beta_{DD}\mu(\mathrm{DI})+\beta_{UD}\mu(\mathrm{UI}),
    \\
    \iota_U(\mu)
    &=
    v_Hq_{\mathrm{inf}}^U+\beta_{UU}\mu(\mathrm{UI})+\beta_{DU}\mu(\mathrm{DI}).
\end{align}
In the ordered basis $(\mathrm{DI},\mathrm{DS},\mathrm{UI},\mathrm{US})$, the infinitesimal generator associated with action $a$ is
\begin{align}
    Q^{\mu,a}
    =
    \begin{pmatrix}
        -q_{\mathrm{rec}}^D-\lambda_{\mathrm{sw}}a & q_{\mathrm{rec}}^D & \lambda_{\mathrm{sw}}a & 0\\
        \iota_D(\mu) & -\iota_D(\mu)-\lambda_{\mathrm{sw}}a & 0 & \lambda_{\mathrm{sw}}a\\
        \lambda_{\mathrm{sw}}a & 0 & -q_{\mathrm{rec}}^U-\lambda_{\mathrm{sw}}a & q_{\mathrm{rec}}^U\\
        0 & \lambda_{\mathrm{sw}}a & \iota_U(\mu) & -\iota_U(\mu)-\lambda_{\mathrm{sw}}a
    \end{pmatrix}.
\end{align}
The discrete-time transition matrix is $P_{\Delta t}^{\mu,a}=\exp(\Delta t Q^{\mu,a})$. The cost of a state is $f(x)=k_D\mathbf 1_{\{x\in\{\mathrm{DI},\mathrm{DS}\}\}}+k_I\mathbf 1_{\{x\in\{\mathrm{DI},\mathrm{UI}\}\}}$,
and the running and terminal rewards are $r_{\Delta t}(x,a,\mu)=-\Delta t f(x)$ and $g_{\Delta t}(x,\mu)=-\Delta t f(x)$. With discount factor $\gamma$, the objective is
\begin{align}
    J_T(\theta;\mu_0)
    =
    \E\left[
        \sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\gamma^t r_{\Delta t}(X_t,A_t,\mu_t^\theta)
        +\gamma^Tg_{\Delta t}(X_T,\mu_T^\theta)
    \right].
\end{align}

\paragraph{Setup.}
The policy is a two-hidden-layer network of width $32$ reading $(t,\mu)$. We use the parameters and the horizon $T=3$ of \cite{meunier2026model}, MF-REINFORCE at $\varepsilon=1$, the reduced transport grid $\lambda\in\{0.1,0.2,0.4\}$ at $\eta=0.85$, and adaptive transport from $(\lambda_0,\eta_0)=(0.2,0.8)$, at the equal budget derived from $B=200$, $n=1$. Training randomizes the initial law over $\operatorname{Dirichlet}(1,1,1,1)$ for $20{,}000$ iterations; validation propagates the frozen policy deterministically from the uniform law over $T_{\mathrm{val}}=50$ steps. Appendix~\ref{appendix:spec-cybersecurity} gives the full specification.

\paragraph{Results.}
Cybersecurity is the first benchmark on which the population-flow derivative buys nothing; Table~\ref{tab:summary-cybersecurity} collects the comparison.

\input{tables/summary_cybersecurity}

REINFORCE reaches $-0.1517\pm0.0000$ within roughly $500$ iterations and stays there, while MF-REINFORCE needs the full $20{,}000$ iterations to approach the same value from below and the transport runs end between $-0.1544$ and $-0.1579$. Figure~\ref{fig:cybersecurity-validation} shows the whole comparison confined to a band of width $0.01$, about $6\%$ of the objective, so the benchmark is best read as a negative result with a small effect size rather than as a failure of the transport estimator. The correction-alignment diagnostic of Section~\ref{sec:correction-alignment} explains it directly: on this benchmark the mean-field correction accounts for one part in a thousand of the gradient and is exactly parallel to the policy-score term, so there is nothing for a population-aware estimator to recover and the extra variance of its auxiliary estimate is pure cost.


\paragraph{Learned state distributions.}
The reason the objectives are so close is visible in the learned flows of Figure~\ref{fig:cybersecurity-flows}. REINFORCE, MF-REINFORCE and fixed-scale transport converge to the same policy: they stop defending altogether, and the population settles on the undefended equilibrium with terminal law $(\mathrm{DI},\mathrm{DS},\mathrm{UI},\mathrm{US})\approx(0.000,0.000,0.438,0.562)$ for REINFORCE and within $0.02$ of it for the other two. On this configuration the defence cost is not worth paying, and three of the four estimators find that out.

The adaptive controller does not. It ends with $79\%$ of the population defended and a terminal law $(0.331,0.462,0.136,0.071)$, so it pays the defence cost throughout and still ends with a slightly larger infected fraction, $0.467$ against $0.438$. This is the same failure recorded in Section~\ref{sec:adaptive-control}: on this benchmark the controller contracts the main scale to $\lambda=0.026$, an order of magnitude below the smallest calibrated value, and the resulting variance moves the policy to a qualitatively different flow rather than merely slowing it down. It is the clearest picture in the suite of what a badly scaled perturbation costs.

\subsection{Distribution Planning}
\label{subsec:distribution-planning}

The distribution-planning benchmark is based on the finite-state control problem used in \cite{meunier2026model,carmona2023model}. The goal is to move a population on a discrete torus towards a prescribed target distribution while penalizing individual movement. This example tests the estimators on a higher-dimensional simplex: the population law has ten coordinates and the policy must coordinate local actions across all sites.

\paragraph{Model.}
The state space is the discrete torus $\Xc=\Z/10\Z=\{0,\ldots,9\}$ and the action space is $\Ac=\{-1,0,+1\}$. Actions move an agent one site left, keep it in place, or move it one site right. The transition is deterministic: $P(x'\mid x,a,\mu) = \mathbf 1_{\{x'=x+a\;(\mathrm{mod}\;10)\}}$.
The target distribution used in the experiments is $\mu_{\mathrm{target}} = (0.02,0.04,0.09,0.16,0.19,0.19,0.16,0.09,0.04,0.02)$.
With movement penalty $c_{\mathrm{mov}}=0.01$, the running and terminal rewards are
\begin{align}
    r(x,a,\mu)
    &=
    -c_{\mathrm{mov}}|a|
    -
    \|\mu-\mu_{\mathrm{target}}\|_2^2,
    \\
    g(x,\mu)
    &=
    -\|\mu-\mu_{\mathrm{target}}\|_2^2.
\end{align}
The horizon is $T=5$ for both training and validation.

\paragraph{Setup.}
The policy is a two-hidden-layer network of width $256$ reading $(t,\mu)$. The horizon is $T=5$. MF-REINFORCE uses $\varepsilon=2$, transport the grid $\lambda\in\{0.1,0.2,0.4\}$ at $\eta=0.85$, and adaptive transport starts from $(\lambda_0,\eta_0)=(0.2,0.8)$, at the equal budget derived from $B=500$, $n=10$. Training randomizes the initial law over $\operatorname{Dirichlet}(\mathbf 1_{10})$ for $30{,}000$ iterations; validation starts from the uniform law. With nine free coordinates this is the largest population argument in the suite. Appendix~\ref{appendix:spec-distribution} gives the full specification.

\paragraph{Results.}
Distribution planning separates the two population-aware estimators more sharply than any other benchmark; Table~\ref{tab:summary-distribution} collects the comparison.

\input{tables/summary_distribution}

MF-REINFORCE reaches $-0.1017\pm0.0161$ within the first few hundred iterations and improves slowly thereafter, whereas REINFORCE is frozen at $-0.2616$ with zero seed dispersion, never leaving the neighbourhood of the uniform law. The transport runs sit between the two but much closer to REINFORCE, at $-0.2421$ for $\lambda=0.4$, and Figure~\ref{fig:distribution-validation} shows why the average is not the whole story: the transport curves oscillate over tens of thousands of iterations without settling, and their seed dispersion is comparable to their distance from REINFORCE. At $\lambda=0.4$ the five seeds end between $-0.1986$ and $-0.2963$, straddling the REINFORCE value of $-0.2616$; no transport seed at any scale comes within $0.09$ of the MF-REINFORCE result.

The qualitative check is nevertheless passed. The right panel of Figure~\ref{fig:distribution-validation} shows that the transport policy moves mass coherently from the uniform law towards the target mode, concentrating it slightly too much on the central sites, rather than producing the local oscillation that a badly scaled sensitivity estimate would cause. The failure is one of accuracy, not of qualitative behaviour.

\subsection{Targeted Advertising}
\label{subsec:targeted-advertising}

The targeted-advertising benchmark is based on the model introduced by Motte and Pham in \cite{motte_pham_mfmdp}. A planner repeatedly decides whether to advertise to a large population in order to increase the proportion of customers while paying an advertising cost. The example is useful for the present study because the mean-field interaction enters through the transition kernel, while the population dynamics reduce to a one-dimensional deterministic recursion.

\paragraph{Model.}
The state and action spaces are $\Xc=\{0,1\}$ and $\Ac=\{0,1\}$. State $x=1$ means that the individual is a customer, and action $a=1$ means that an advertisement is displayed. Writing $p=\mu(1)$ for the customer proportion, the transition kernel is $P(1\mid x,a,\mu) = \min\{p+\kappa_{\mathrm{ad}}a,1\}, \qquad P(0\mid x,a,\mu) = 1-P(1\mid x,a,\mu)$.
Thus advertising increases the probability of being a customer by $\kappa_{\mathrm{ad}}$, up to saturation at $1$, while the dependence on $p$ models positive social influence. The one-step reward is $r(x,a,\mu)=x-c_{\mathrm{ad}}a$,
and the terminal reward is zero. The finite-horizon discounted objective is $J_T(\theta;p_0) = \E\left[ \sum_{t=0}^{T-1} \gamma^t(X_t^\theta-c_{\mathrm{ad}}A_t^\theta) \right]$.

\paragraph{Setup.}
The learned policy is a two-hidden-layer network of width $32$ reading $(t,\mu)$, at horizon $T=5$. MF-REINFORCE uses $\varepsilon=1$, transport the grid $\lambda\in\{0.1,0.2,0.4\}$ at $\eta=0.85$, and adaptive transport starts from $(\lambda_0,\eta_0)=(0.2,0.8)$, at the equal budget derived from $B=200$, $n=10$. Training randomizes the initial customer share over $\Uc([0.05,0.95])$ for $10{,}000$ iterations; validation averages over a fixed grid in the same interval. Appendix~\ref{appendix:spec-advertising} gives the full specification.

\paragraph{Results.}
Targeted advertising is a plateau-escape problem. REINFORCE converges within a few hundred iterations to $0.9688\pm0.0000$ and never moves again: its learned policy advertises with probability exactly $0$ in both states and at every time, so the customer share never leaves its initial value, while the reference policy $\widehat q$ advertises with probability $1$ almost everywhere. The policy that never advertises is a stationary point of the detached objective, and REINFORCE cannot leave it. Every population-aware estimator does leave it, and the ordering of Table~\ref{tab:objective-summary} follows: MF-REINFORCE reaches $0.9946\pm0.0091$, fixed-scale transport at $\lambda=0.1$ reaches $0.9795\pm0.0089$, and adaptive transport $0.9727\pm0.0078$.

Neither estimator recovers the reference policy, and they fail in different ways. Table~\ref{tab:advertising-policies} gives the learned advertising probability in each of the two states. MF-REINFORCE advertises to everyone with probability $1$ for the first two steps and then switches off completely, which brings the customer share from $0.2$ to $0.603$ and holds it there; the reference keeps advertising until the end. Transport and adaptive transport instead learn a state-targeted policy that advertises to non-customers with probability $1$ and to customers with probability $0$, at every step. That policy is intuitively reasonable, since advertising to an existing customer costs without converting anyone, and it drives the customer share higher than MF-REINFORCE does, to $0.738$; it nonetheless scores lower, because the reference advertises to customers as well and the retention this buys outweighs its cost.

\section{Continuous-state space benchmarks}

Both benchmarks below are exact-chart instances of the construction of Section~\ref{sec:continuous-state-space}, in the sense discussed there: the perturbed population argument is one-dimensional, given by the mean, the chart is the closed moment recursion rather than a fitted Gaussian mixture, and the representation error vanishes by construction. The higher moments are still propagated, but only to evaluate the exact objective and the reference optimum at a frozen policy. They therefore test the perturbation of the population argument and the auxiliary sensitivity recursion in isolation, with no mixture-fitting error superposed on them.


\subsection{Linear--Quadratic}
\label{subsec:linear-quadratic}

We first consider a scalar linear--quadratic mean-field control problem. This benchmark is deliberately transparent: the population law is Gaussian, the moment recursion is closed, the perturbed objective and its gradient are available in closed form, and the unperturbed optimizer is given by a Riccati recursion. It therefore provides a continuous-state analogue of the two-state benchmark, where optimization errors can be separated from estimator bias and Monte Carlo variance.

\paragraph{Model.}
The initial state is $X_0\sim\Nc(\mu_0,\Sigma_0)$ and the action space is $\R$. For a population law with mean $\bar m_t$, the policy is Gaussian,
\begin{align}
    \pi_t^\theta(\cdot\mid x,m)
    =
    \Nc\left(\theta_t^1x+\theta_t^2\bar m_t,\tau^2\right),
    \qquad
    \theta_t=(\theta_t^1,\theta_t^2)\in\R^2.
\end{align}
The controlled dynamics are $X_{t+1} = aX_t+bA_t+c\bar m_t+\sigma \varepsilon_{t+1}, \qquad \varepsilon_{t+1}\sim\Nc(0,1)$.
The implementation uses rewards equal to the negative of the quadratic cost. Equivalently, validation minimizes
\begin{align}
    C_T(\theta)
    =
    \E\left[
        \sum_{t=0}^{T-1}
        \left(qX_t^2+rA_t^2+\gamma \bar m_t^2\right)
        +q_TX_T^2+\gamma_T\bar m_T^2
    \right],
\end{align}
and reports the validation reward $-C_T(\theta)$.

\paragraph{Setup.}
The unperturbed optimizer is given by a Riccati recursion, and the perturbed objective and its gradient are available in closed form. The horizon is $T=20$. The population argument is perturbed affinely, $M_t^{\lambda,\theta}=(1+\lambda\zeta_t)\mu_t^\theta+\lambda\beta_t$ with $\zeta_t,\beta_t$ i.i.d.\ $\Nc(0,\rho^2)$; the fixed-scale grid is $\lambda\in\{0.05,0.1,0.2,0.4,0.8\}$ at $\eta=0.85$ and adaptive transport starts from $(\lambda_0,\eta_0)=(0.2,0.8)$. The reference budget is $B=200$ main and $n=1$ law-gradient trajectories, which transport reallocates to $n_{\mathrm{law}}=20$ auxiliary trajectories at the same total cost. Both exact moment flow and particle flow are run. Appendix~\ref{appendix:spec-lq} gives the model, the parameters and the diagnostics protocol.

\paragraph{Results.}
The Riccati optimizer gives $C(\theta^\star)=7.2238$ in the cost convention. REINFORCE ends at $7.5157\pm0.0010$ and fixed-scale transport at $\lambda=0.1$ at $7.2952\pm0.0023$, closing $75.5\%$ of the REINFORCE optimality gap with a seed dispersion two orders of magnitude below the effect. On the full training axis this is invisible, and the comparison even looks favourable to REINFORCE, which comes within $0.05$ of its final value at iteration $3{,}160$ against $8{,}530$ for transport, and captures $99.35\%$ of the available improvement against $99.85\%$. The left panel of Figure~\ref{fig:lq-convergence} zooms on the last $6{,}000$ iterations and shows what that hides: the REINFORCE curve is not converged but oscillating between $7.38$ and $7.53$, while the transport curves descend smoothly and are still improving at the end of the budget. The two estimators do not approach the same limit.

\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/lq_convergence.png}

Because the perturbed objective and its optimizer are both available in closed form here, the residual error splits into two interpretable pieces, reported in Table~\ref{tab:lq-decomposition}. The perturbation bias $|J^\lambda(\theta_\lambda^\star)-J(\theta^\star)|$ is a property of the problem the estimator targets rather than of the run, and grows from $0.275$ at $\lambda=0.05$ to $70.4$ at $\lambda=0.8$; the optimization error $|J^\lambda(\widehat\theta_\lambda)-J^\lambda(\theta_\lambda^\star)|$ measures how far the learned parameter is from the optimizer of that perturbed problem and is the only part a better estimator could remove, staying at $0.073$--$0.092$ for $\lambda\leq0.2$ before growing to $0.42$. The optimality gap in the last column is not their sum: a policy optimized for a heavily biased $J^\lambda$ is displaced from $\theta^\star$ by far less than the bias of the objective, which is why $\lambda=0.8$ carries a perturbation bias of $70.4$ and a gap of only $0.19$. Together the three columns show a trade-off with an interior minimum at $\lambda=0.1$, plotted in the right panel of Figure~\ref{fig:lq-convergence}, whose two mechanisms Section~\ref{sec:theory-validation} measures directly.

\input{tables/lq_decomposition}

This is also where the particle approximation is cheapest: replacing the exact moment recursion \eqref{eq:lq-moment-recursion} by particle estimates moves the final cost at $\lambda=0.1$ from $7.29525$ to $7.29597$, about $1\%$ of the remaining gap, and Figure~\ref{fig:lq-flow-comparison} shows the two curves indistinguishable over the whole run. The contrast with the two-state benchmark, where the same substitution costs two thirds of the gap, is dimensional: here the population law enters only through its mean, and a mean is what a particle ensemble estimates well.


\subsection{Mean--Variance Portfolio}
\label{subsec:mean-variance-portfolio}

We next consider a discrete-time mean--variance portfolio selection problem. As in the LQ benchmark, the relevant moments satisfy closed recursions and the exact perturbed objective is available, but the state is driven by multiplicative asset returns. This makes the example a useful continuous-state test in which the estimator noise comes from financial returns and the population dependence is carried both by the feedback policy and by an explicit running mean-field penalty.

\paragraph{Model.}
Let $X_t\in\R$ denote the wealth of an investor at time $t$ and let $\alpha_t\in\R$ denote the monetary amount invested in the risky asset, the remainder being held at the deterministic gross risk-free return $s_t>0$. Writing $R_{t+1}$ for the excess return of the risky asset over $[t,t+1]$, with mean $\bar r_t$ and variance $\sigma_{R,t}^2$, the wealth dynamics are
\begin{align}
    X_{t+1}=s_tX_t+\alpha_tR_{t+1},
    \qquad
    t=0,\ldots,T-1.
    \label{eq:portfolio-wealth-dynamics}
\end{align}
The investor is subject to a precommitment mean--variance criterion on terminal wealth, penalized by a running cost on the aggregate position of the population. With $\mu_t=\E[X_t]$ the population mean, the running and terminal rewards are
\begin{align}
    r(x,a,\mu)=-\gamma\mu^2,
    \qquad
    g(x,\mu)=x-\chi(x-\mu)^2,
    \label{eq:portfolio-rewards}
\end{align}
where $\chi>0$ is the risk-aversion coefficient and $\gamma\geq0$ is a mean-field penalty. The terminal reward yields the usual mean--variance objective $\E[X_T]-\chi\operatorname{Var}(X_T)$ at the consistent population law, while the running term introduces an explicit dependence on the population mean. In the current benchmark we set $\gamma=2$, so the task contains a genuine population-gradient component in addition to the dependence of the feedback policy on the mean.

\paragraph{Setup.}
The mean and variance recursions are closed, so the exact objective and the optimal $(k_t^\star,\ell_t^\star)$ of \eqref{eq:portfolio-optimal-k}--\eqref{eq:portfolio-optimal-l} are available for validation. The horizon is $T=10$. The population mean is perturbed as in the linear--quadratic benchmark, with the smaller grid $\lambda\in\{0.025,0.05,0.1,0.2,0.4\}$ at $\eta=0.85$ to match the scale of the wealth process. The reference budget is $B=500$, $n=1$, i.e.\ $5{,}110$ simulated transitions per update, which transport reallocates to $n_{\mathrm{law}}=200$ auxiliary trajectories. Appendix~\ref{appendix:spec-portfolio} gives the full specification.

\paragraph{Results.}
The portfolio benchmark produces the largest separation of the two continuous-state examples, and it is the only one on which the adaptive controller is the best method in the comparison. Against the closed-form optimum $J(\theta^\star)=-13.133$, REINFORCE ends at $-20.535\pm0.004$, fixed-scale transport between $-18.195$ and $-17.980$, and adaptive transport at $-17.193\pm0.594$, closing respectively $34.5\%$ and $45.1\%$ of the REINFORCE gap. Figure~\ref{fig:portfolio-validation} shows that REINFORCE does not stall but degrades: it drifts below its own initialization from iteration $2{,}000$ onwards and ends more than one unit below where it started, whereas every transport variant climbs.


Two features qualify the result, both about what five seeds establish.

First, the choice of $\lambda$ barely matters here: across the whole grid the final objective moves only from $-18.19$ to $-17.98$, a range of $0.21$, against a distance to REINFORCE of about $2.5$. What produces the improvement is using the population-flow derivative at all, not the scale at which it is estimated. This is the opposite of the two-state benchmark, where the same grid spans more than six units of objective and the choice of $\lambda$ decides whether the run succeeds.

Second, the seed dispersion exceeds that grid range: each fixed scale carries a standard deviation between $1.08$ and $1.29$ against the $0.21$ separating best from worst. Five seeds therefore cannot rank the $\lambda$ values, and the apparent ordering inside the transport block of Table~\ref{tab:objective-summary} should not be read as one. The comparison against REINFORCE is a different matter, since a gap of $2.5$ against a dispersion of $1.2$ is resolved, and in the same direction on every seed.

The same decomposition as on the linear--quadratic benchmark falls out very differently here. Table~\ref{tab:portfolio-decomposition} shows a perturbation bias that is small at every scale, from $0.03$ at $\lambda=0.025$ to $7.35$ at $\lambda=0.4$, against an optimization error that never drops below $4.97$: the learned policy is far from the optimizer of the perturbed problem it is actually solving, not merely displaced by the bias of that problem. The limitation here is estimator variance, not perturbation bias, consistent with the auxiliary sample size of $200$ per coordinate being the largest in the suite and still insufficient.

The controlled ablation of Section~\ref{sec:correction-alignment}, which switches the explicit population dependence on and off through $\gamma$, attributes the improvement to that dependence rather than to the perturbation acting as a generic exploration device.

\section{Adaptive perturbation control}
\label{sec:adaptive-control}

The fixed-scale results above are read at the best entry of a grid costing between three and twenty runs per benchmark. The adaptive controller of Algorithm~\ref{alg:adaptive-transport} exists to remove that grid, and the question is whether one run from the benchmark-independent initialization $(\lambda_0,\eta_0)=(0.2,0.8)$ reaches a comparable objective. Table~\ref{tab:adaptive-scales} reports the scales it selects.

\input{tables/adaptive_scales}

The controller contracts both scales on every benchmark: mildly on the continuous-state problems, where $\lambda$ settles between $0.20$ and $0.60$, and strongly on the finite-state ones, where it reaches $0.025$--$0.040$. The auxiliary scale is contracted everywhere, from $\eta_0=0.8$ to between $0.20$ and $0.68$, which is the one systematic disagreement with the fixed-scale evidence, since the calibration grid prefers $\eta$ near the top of its range. Figure~\ref{fig:adaptive-schedule} shows the mechanism on the linear--quadratic benchmark: the balance signals $z_\lambda$ and $z_\eta$ are negative at almost every checkpoint, meaning the debiased bias estimate is small relative to the measured variance, and the controller shrinks the scale carrying that variance. For $\lambda$ this is the correct response and the trajectory is non-monotone, rising to $0.32$ before returning to $0.226$; for $\eta$ the same signal drives a monotone contraction that the grid says is unhelpful.

Measured on the objective rather than on the scales, the controller does most of what it is meant to do. It recovers $97\%$ of the benefit of the grid from a single run on the linear--quadratic benchmark, reaching $-7.3021$ against $-7.2952$; it is the best method outright on the portfolio benchmark; it lands between the fixed grid and MF-REINFORCE on two-state at both horizons; and it escapes the REINFORCE plateau on targeted advertising.

The two failures are cybersecurity, where it is the worst method at $-0.1602$, and distribution planning, where it reaches $-0.5596$ against $-0.2616$ for REINFORCE and $-0.2421$ for the best fixed scale. The terminal scale alone does not distinguish them: the controller contracts $\lambda$ to $0.026$ and $0.025$ there, but to $0.039$ and $0.040$ on two-state and advertising where it performs acceptably, and all four lie below $\lambda=0.05$, the smallest calibrated scale, so it leaves the calibrated range on every finite-state benchmark. What separates the two pairs is the dimension of the population argument, a two-point simplex on two-state and advertising against a four-point and a ten-point one. The perturbation-density score has $N-1$ coordinates and scales as $\lambda^{-1}$, so contracting the main scale inflates a variance that grows with the dimension, and the same terminal $\lambda$ is harmless on $\Delta_2$ and damaging on $\Delta_{10}$.

The controller is not failing to measure: the bias estimate is resolved on $68\%$ and $72\%$ of checkpoints on these two benchmarks, the highest rates in the suite, against $48\%$ on the linear--quadratic benchmark where the outcome is good. It measures a small bias correctly and draws the wrong conclusion from it, because the balance rule weighs bias against variance without accounting for the $\lambda^{-1}$ growth of the score.

\paragraph{The perturbation is no larger than $\lambda$.}
Since $M_t^{\lambda,\theta}=(1-\lambda)\mu_t^\theta+\lambda q_t$ with $q_t\in\Delta_N$, we have $d_{\mathrm{TV}}(M_t^{\lambda,\theta},\mu_t^\theta)=\lambda\,d_{\mathrm{TV}}(q_t,\mu_t^\theta)\leq\lambda$, the estimate every subsequent bound is built on. Table~\ref{tab:tv-bounds} records the largest value along the learned flow of every finite-state transport run; the ratio to $\lambda$ reaches $0.80$ on two-state and advertising and $1.00$ on distribution planning, so the perturbation is neither degenerate nor larger than the theory allows.


\paragraph{The perturbed optimum converges at the predicted rate.}
Theorem~\ref{theorem:discrete-convergence-objective} gives $|J^\lambda(\theta)-J(\theta)|\leq C_T\lambda$ uniformly in $\theta$, and its corollary transfers this to the optimizers. On the linear--quadratic and portfolio benchmarks both $J^\lambda$ and its maximizer are available in closed form, so the induced error on the optimal value is computed exactly rather than estimated. It satisfies the bound with room to spare, at a rate faster than the generic one: dividing by $\lambda^2$ gives a constant to within $0.1\%$ over a sixteen-fold range of $\lambda$. Both objectives are quadratic in the population argument, so the linear term of the expansion vanishes at the optimum and the residual is $O(\lambda^2)$; the measurement recovers exactly that, which checks the derivation rather than contradicting the bound.


\paragraph{The estimator is unbiased for the perturbed gradient.}
Proposition~\ref{prop:oracle-mse-decomposition} states that the estimator is exactly unbiased for $\nabla_\theta J^\lambda$ under oracle sensitivities, and Theorem~\ref{theorem:discrete-mse-decomposition} adds the bias the estimated sensitivity introduces, of order $\lambda^{-1}\sqrt{\eta^2+(n\eta^2)^{-1}}$. The distinction is testable: at a frozen policy, replicate the estimator and compare its mean with the closed-form $\nabla_\theta J^\lambda$. Table~\ref{tab:gradient-diagnostics-lq} does this on the linear--quadratic benchmark with $50$ replications at $8\,192$ particles. The bias is indistinguishable from zero at $\lambda\leq0.2$, marginal at $\lambda=0.4$ with $p=0.12$, and unambiguous at $\lambda=0.8$: the prediction holds in the regime the theory targets, and departs exactly where the $\lambda^{-1}$ prefactor on the auxiliary error is largest relative to the signal.


The portfolio benchmark, Table~\ref{tab:gradient-diagnostics-portfolio} in Appendix~\ref{appendix:portfolio-diagnostics}, is the honest limit of the measurement rather than of the theory: the estimator dispersion is $347$ against an exact gradient of norm $1.08$, a signal-to-noise ratio of $0.02$ per replication, and the measured bias of $50$ is indistinguishable from zero at every scale. The test is uninformative there, not passed.


\paragraph{The gradient bias and the mean-square error decompose as stated.}
The same tables check the two remaining predictions. The gradient perturbation bias, which Theorem~\ref{theorem:discrete-gradient-convergence} bounds by $C_T\lambda$, scales as $\lambda^2$ with a coefficient between $0.92$ and $1.18$ on the linear--quadratic benchmark and between $2.46$ and $2.48$ on the portfolio benchmark, constant to within $1\%$ across the grid. This is the gradient-level counterpart of the objective-level scaling above, measured on a different quantity by a different route, so the two are independent checks.

The mean-square error of Theorem~\ref{theorem:discrete-mse-decomposition} splits into a squared bias and a dispersion, and the measured quantities satisfy that identity run by run to within the $(R-1)/R$ factor of the unbiased dispersion estimate. Reading the two contributions against each other makes the trade-off concrete: at $\lambda=0.05$ the dispersion is $0.59$ against a squared bias of $0.0018$, so the error is essentially all variance, whereas at $\lambda=0.8$ the dispersion has grown to $4.78$ and the bias to $0.96$, and both terms have deteriorated together, with the cosine alignment falling monotonically from $0.94$ to $0.64$. This is the quantitative form of the interior optimum in $\lambda$ that Table~\ref{tab:lq-decomposition} shows on the objective.

\paragraph{The size of the correction, measured on the estimator itself.}
The same replications give the size of the transport correction relative to the policy-score term, measured on the estimator rather than on an oracle. On the linear--quadratic benchmark it accounts for a fraction $1.27$ to $1.58$ of the gradient norm at $\lambda\leq0.2$, falling to $0.24$ at $\lambda=0.8$, and is resolved against its own replication noise at every scale below $0.4$. On the portfolio benchmark the measured fraction is $1.00$ at every scale with a $\chi^2$ $p$-value above $0.96$, so it is not distinguishable from noise at the saved policies. This does not contradict the training results but confirms the point of Section~\ref{sec:correction-alignment}, that the magnitude of the correction is not by itself informative; the evidence that the population channel drives the portfolio improvement comes from the controlled $\gamma$ ablation.

\paragraph{What is not tested.}
Three limits are worth recording. The uniformity in $\theta$ of Theorem~\ref{theorem:discrete-convergence-objective} is tested only at the policies the runs actually reach, which concentrate near the optimum, so nothing is said about the constants far from it. The auxiliary error bound of Proposition~\ref{prop:auxiliary-sensitivity-error}, $C_T(\eta^2+(n\eta^2)^{-1})$, is the one prediction these artifacts cannot isolate, since separating its two terms would need a sweep in $n$ at a fixed policy; the calibration grid shows only the combined effect through the training outcome. And the replicated diagnostics exist only on the two benchmarks exposing a closed-form oracle and a directly parametrized policy, the finite-state benchmarks having network policies for which an automatic-differentiation oracle for $\nabla_\theta J^\lambda$ is implemented at $\lambda=0$ only.

\section{Mean-field correction diagnostic}
\label{sec:correction-alignment}

The benchmarks above do not order the estimators consistently, although the mean-field structure is present in all of them. This section isolates the quantity that separates the regimes: not whether the population-flow term is large, but whether it is collinear with the term REINFORCE already computes.

REINFORCE never differentiates the law supplied to the policy, the reward or the transition, so its target is the partial derivative of the objective at a frozen population flow, whereas the transport estimator targets the total derivative. Both are computable whenever the environment admits a differentiable evaluation path, here the exact population recursion on the finite-state benchmarks and a pathwise rollout under common random numbers on the continuous ones. Writing $\mu^\theta$ for the population flow, define
\begin{align}
    G_{\mathrm{full}}(\theta) &= \nabla_\theta J\!\left(\theta,\mu^\theta\right), &
    G_{\mathrm{det}}(\theta) &= \nabla_\theta J\!\left(\theta,\mu\right)\Big|_{\mu=\mu^\theta},
    \label{eq:two-gradients}
\end{align}
where the second expression detaches $\mu$ from the computational graph before differentiating, so that the population channel is suppressed. Their difference is exactly the mean-field correction the transport estimator is built to recover, and $G_{\mathrm{det}}$ is what REINFORCE estimates; both are evaluation-only and never available during training. The natural summary, the relative size $\|G_{\mathrm{full}}-G_{\mathrm{det}}\|/\|G_{\mathrm{full}}\|$, is not predictive: the policies are optimized with Adam, whose update is invariant to a positive rescaling of the gradient, so if the correction is parallel to $G_{\mathrm{det}}$, then $G_{\mathrm{full}}=(1+c)\,G_{\mathrm{det}},\qquad c>-1$,
the two estimators induce the same update no matter how large $c$ is: a correction can account for half the gradient norm and be worth nothing. What changes the optimization path is the component orthogonal to $G_{\mathrm{det}}$, captured by the alignment
\begin{align}
    \varrho(\theta)
    =
    \frac{\left\langle G_{\mathrm{full}}(\theta),\,G_{\mathrm{det}}(\theta)\right\rangle}
    {\left\|G_{\mathrm{full}}(\theta)\right\|\left\|G_{\mathrm{det}}(\theta)\right\|}.
    \label{eq:correction-alignment}
\end{align}
Values close to $+1$ mean REINFORCE is directionally correct and the correction only rescales the step; values at or below zero mean it ascends a direction that is not an ascent direction for the true objective.

\paragraph{Measured alignment.}
We evaluate \eqref{eq:correction-alignment} at a reference policy obtained by running REINFORCE for $300$ iterations from the benchmark initialization, so the diagnostic is read away from the initial point but before any estimator has converged, and repeat over five independent reference policies. Table~\ref{tab:correction-alignment} reports the mean and standard deviation together with the ordering observed in training.

The alignments fall into two separated groups, with nothing between $0.092$ and $0.985$. Every benchmark on which REINFORCE fails outright has $\varrho\leq0.092$: it descends away from its initialization on two-state and portfolio, and freezes at a stationary point of the detached objective on distribution planning and advertising. The two benchmarks with $\varrho\geq0.985$ are exactly those on which REINFORCE is directionally competitive, which is a weaker statement than being best: on the linear--quadratic benchmark the correction is far from negligible in norm, accounting for $80\%$ of the gradient, yet nearly parallel to the policy-score term and therefore nearly invisible to a scale-invariant optimizer, and transport still ends ahead. It is the distance from $+1$, not the sign of $\varrho$, that carries the information: distribution planning has $\varrho=+0.092\pm0.075$ and still defeats REINFORCE, since a correction nearly orthogonal to the policy score reorients the update as effectively as one that opposes it.

\paragraph{What $\varrho\approx+1$ does and does not certify.}
The two high-alignment benchmarks do not behave identically, and the difference bounds what the diagnostic can claim. On cybersecurity REINFORCE is the best method outright; on the linear--quadratic benchmark it is not, since transport closes $75.5\%$ of the optimality gap with a seed dispersion two orders of magnitude below the effect. A high alignment therefore certifies that REINFORCE ascends a useful direction, but not that the two estimators share a limit: the alignment is strictly below one, leaving an orthogonal component of size $\sqrt{1-\varrho^2}=0.17$, and REINFORCE indeed oscillates around a biased point rather than converging, as the left panel of Figure~\ref{fig:lq-convergence} shows. On cybersecurity the correction is not merely aligned but negligible, $\varrho=+1.000$ to six decimals with a correction fraction of $0.001$, so the two gradients have the same zeros to numerical precision. The reading is therefore ordinal: $\varrho\leq0.1$ predicts that REINFORCE fails, $\varrho\approx+1$ that it is competitive, and the size of the residual gap is governed by the orthogonal component, which the alignment alone does not resolve.

\paragraph{Controlled pairs.}
The portfolio problem turns the observation above into a controlled comparison, since the market-impact coefficient $\gamma$ multiplies the only reward term that depends explicitly on the population and can be switched off with the dynamics held fixed.

At $\gamma=0$ the terminal reward depends on the population only through the centered variable $x-\mu$, so $\partial_\mu\E[-\chi(X-\mu)^2]=2\chi(\E[X]-\mu)=0$ at the consistent law, for every risk-aversion level. The measured gradients confirm that the correction vanishes, $\|G_{\mathrm{full}}\|=\|G_{\mathrm{det}}\|=0.083$ with $\varrho=+1.000$ to three decimals across all five replications, so REINFORCE is unbiased on this instance and transport can only add variance. That is what training shows: at $\gamma=0$ transport ends below its own initialization while REINFORCE recovers $42\%$ of the available objective range, and at $\gamma=2$ the ordering reverses. The same pair shows why the magnitude diagnostic misleads, since the correction measured on the estimator accounts for $83\%$ of the gradient at $\gamma=0$ even though the exact correction is zero; that $83\%$ is entirely Monte Carlo noise from the auxiliary estimate.

\paragraph{Practical use and limitations.}
The alignment costs two automatic-differentiation passes over one deterministic evaluation and needs no closed-form oracle, so it can be computed on any benchmark with a differentiable evaluation path before committing to a training grid. It is nonetheless local, read at reference policies after a fixed number of REINFORCE iterations, with no guarantee of being constant along a trajectory; group membership never changes across the five replications, but distribution planning ranges from $+0.021$ to $+0.217$ while cybersecurity and advertising are stable to within $0.023$. Finally, this is an observation over six benchmarks and one controlled pair, not a theorem: a low alignment indicates that the correction can change the optimization path, not that a model-free estimator of it has low enough variance to exploit it. The portfolio runs at $\gamma=2$, where transport improves on REINFORCE only after the auxiliary sample size is raised from $1$ to $200$, show that the two requirements are separate.
